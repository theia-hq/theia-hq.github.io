You are your key.

Your identity is a key you hold, not an account someone can close. Reach your machines by it, give one service to one person, and take it back.

// The idea

Most ways to reach your own machines start with an account on someone else's server, and that server decides who gets in. Theia starts from the key.

  1. 01

    Nothing to sign up for, nothing to take away.

    Your key lives on your machine. There is no account, so there is nothing for a company to suspend, delete or deplatform.

  2. 02

    Your machine decides who gets in.

    Every connection proves the key it comes from. Your machine checks that key against what you granted, on the spot, and asks no server.

  3. 03

    Access is given, and taken back.

    You give one service, not your whole machine or network. It ends on the date you set, or sooner when you revoke it, and everyone else keeps theirs.

  4. 04

    The only operator is you.

    Machines connect directly, or through a relay that cannot read the traffic or let anyone in. The default relays and address lookup are n0's, so n0 sees which machines talk to each other. You can run both.

  1. 01Today

    Your machines, as one.

    Every machine you own answers to one key.

    A new laptop joins with one invite and reaches everything your other machines serve: a shell with no ssh keys to copy, a folder that takes files, any local port. Lose it, and you revoke it on each machine it reached.

  2. 02Today

    One service, one person, until a date.

    Give a friend one thing on your machine, not your machine.

    Let a friend into the photo library on your home server until Sunday. Take it back on Thursday, and their next connection is refused; a session they had open closes within about a second. The same link in a stranger's hands opens nothing.

  3. 03Designed

    Any network, or none.

    A route through family, or over Bluetooth with no internet at all.

    Your sister's laptop on a café network will reach your desktop at home through your mum's machine, because your mum will let family pass through her machine. When the power and the internet are out, two laptops in the same room will still reach each other by key over Bluetooth.

  4. 04Designed

    Your family, granted once.

    Access for people, not machines, with every device they own included.

    You will make a group of your mum, your dad, your brother and your sister, and give it the family photo server in one line. Every machine you own will honour it, and each of them will get in from any of their devices.

  5. 05Coming

    Mail at your key.

    An email address made of your key, at any gateway, delivered to your own machine.

    Addressbf01i6u56y6gdf4a74w7vy4vebtc6gqbktmqe3ljxdoha2jlfc4zdhba@gateway.example

    Anyone will write to it from an ordinary mail client. The part before the @ is your key, so any gateway will carry it, one you run or one you trust. Change gateways, and the mail will still land on your machine. The gateway will see each message as it arrives, as any mail server does, then seal it to your key. If your machine is off, another machine of yours will hold it sealed until you are back.

  6. 06Coming

    Drives.

    Your files in any browser, stored wherever you choose.

    Your photos will live on your own disk, in storage you pay for, or on your brother's server across town. Anything that leaves your machine will be encrypted first. You will open them from any browser by key, and give a friend one folder for a week the same way you give a service.

  7. 07Designed

    Try it in your browser.

    Theia inside a browser tab, with nothing installed.

    A link will open a tab that is itself a Theia machine. The link will carry a one-time invite, and the tab will open the file or the shell it was given: your own machine's shell, on a borrowed laptop. Closing the tab will leave nothing behind.

The rest of the plan

Today

  • Fix your mum's computer from anywhere. Set her machine up once on a visit; after that it answers to your key, and you reach its shell whenever it breaks, with no remote-desktop account.
  • A key you can lock. Seal your key under a passphrase, back it up to a USB stick, and restore it on a new machine.

Building

  • Touch ID. On a Mac, your key will open with a touch.
  • Names on every device. Save a contact once, and every machine you own will know it.
  • CI runners you reach by key. Step into a failing build's runner by key, for the life of the job.
  • A machine that sleeps until you call it. A machine on GitHub that will start when you call it, let you in by key, and shut down when its time is up.

Designed

  • A machine to try it on. A machine we run, open to anyone, will answer your first command the minute you install.
  • Work and home, kept apart. One key for both, with nothing crossing between them; when you leave, work will revoke you once, everywhere.
  • Recovery, when a key is lost. Ten words, written down once, will move you to a new key, and the people who saved your key will follow you to it.
  • When the network is blocked. Your machines will reach a blocked relay and lookup through a Tor client you run, and nothing else changes.
  • VPN mode and exits. Reach your home server's web apps by address, and send your traffic out through a friend's machine.
  • Build on the same parts. Your own service will get the same grants and revocation, with no access checks to write.

Coming

  • A protocol, not only a program. Every format written down, so a stranger's program built from the specs will reach yours.
  • Send to someone who is away. A file sent to your sister while her laptop is shut will wait, sealed, on one of her own machines.
  • Documents, kept in step. A notes file you and your sister both edit, shared and taken back like everything else.
  • For people with nothing installed. A plain link that will open in a browser or in the ssh they already have, and a public web address for one service.
  • Names in any app. Type desk.alice into ssh or a browser; a web domain you own will point to your key.
  • Apps on every platform. A desktop app, and Theia on Windows, Android and iPhone.
  • Agents. Give an AI agent one tool for an hour, with a budget, and end it sooner if you like.
  • Run code at a key. Sandboxed code that will wake when someone with a grant calls, and cost nothing in between.
  • A market of exits. Exits anyone can offer, and anyone can pick.
  • And more. Hardware security keys as locks. Access your family will receive if you die. Which of your machines are online, at a glance. A file from your phone to your laptop with one tap. Webhooks reachable only by the right sender.

// Where we are today

swoosh is the program you install, for Linux and macOS.

What works today

  • Across NATs: ping, speed, ssh, send, reach, fetch.
  • Everything you serve is gated: your devices and the people you grant get in; --public opens it to anyone.
  • Add your own machines with invite add and adopt.
  • Share one service until a date with grant issue.
  • grant revoke takes it back; open sessions close within about a second.
  • Seal, back up and restore your key: identity protect, export, restore.

// The libraries

swoosh is the program; the rest are the libraries it is built from.

Each library does one job and works on its own, in your own Rust program.

  • swoosh Reaches a machine by its public key instead of its IP address: ping it, measure the link, ssh in, send files, reach the services it serves, and share access.
  • bifrost Opens a byte stream to a machine by its public key, wherever it is on the internet, across NATs, without knowing its address.
  • nauthy Capability tokens you can narrow, hand on and revoke with no server, rooted at one ed25519 key you hold and checked offline.
  • tightbeam Serves named services behind one gate at a public key; a peer that passes the gate gets a byte stream to the one service it asked for.
  • services Service engines that each do one job for a caller already let in: fetch a web page, measure a link, serve a shell, transfer files.
  • keystore Keeps one ed25519 secret key in one file, plain or sealed under a passphrase or a Mac's Touch ID.
  • quirk A QUIC-style transport over UDP, written from scratch to learn how one works. It does not speak standard QUIC.
